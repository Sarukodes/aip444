## 2. Main Claims

The guide makes the following key claims and statements:

1. **Agentic AI is distinct from generative AI**: Agentic AI systems not only generate content but also *take action*, sequence steps, interact with digital systems, and pursue defined goals within boundaries — unlike generative AI which is reactive and content-focused.

2. **Agentic AI presents heightened risks**: Because it can act autonomously across multiple systems and permissions, agentic AI introduces risks including unintended system interactions, misaligned task execution, and the need for stronger oversight and auditability.

3. **Two core governance principles**: The guide establishes **Bounded Autonomy** (AI agents should operate within tight, explicit parameters limiting data access, tools, permissions, and scope) and **Recoverability** (AI agents should be designed so they can be paused, stopped, and returned to a safe state, with all actions fully logged).

4. **Four-level autonomy spectrum**: The guide describes an assistive-to-adaptive spectrum with four levels: assistive, semi-autonomous, high autonomy, and adaptive autonomy.

5. **Human oversight is prerequisite**: Public servants must receive appropriate training and maintain relevant skill sets to provide meaningful oversight of agentic AI systems.

6. **Existing policy frameworks apply**: The guide extends — and remains fully aligned with — the Guide on the Use of Generative AI, the Directive on Automated Decision-Making, the Policy on Service and Digital, and the Directive on Security Management.

## 3. Author Investigation

### Author Identity

The document is attributed to the **Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat**, specifically the Office of the Chief Information Officer of Canada. No individual author name is prominently displayed on the page, which is standard practice for official government policy documents. The Responsible Data and AI team (ai-ia@tbs-sct.gc.ca) is credited for enquiries.

### Institutional Authority

The Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat (TBS) is a federal department with statutory authority over government administration, digital policy, and information management. TBS's Office of the Chief Information Officer (OCIO) oversees digital government innovations including AI governance. This institutional mandate is well-established and has been exercising oversight over government AI use since at least 2019 with the Directive on Automated Decision-Making.

### Expertise Assessment

TBS has demonstrated sustained expertise in AI governance through:
- The **Directive on Automated Decision-Making** (effective April 1, 2019), which established mandatory Algorithmic Impact Assessments for automated decision systems.
- The **Guide on the Use of Generative AI** (published September 2023, updated February 2024).
- Collaboration with the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security on AI security guidance.
- Partnerships with the School of Public Service for AI training courses.

### Conflicts of Interest

No conflicts of interest were identified. The publication is funded by public tax revenues through a federal government department.

## 7. Contradicting Evidence

No credible evidence directly contradicting the guide's main claims was found. The independent review by Fractional Pros Canada identified several gaps and technical imprecisions (see Section 9), but these do not constitute direct contradictions — they represent areas where the guide could be more comprehensive or precise.

Specific gaps noted by reviewers (not contradictions):
- Minor definitional imprecision in the glossary regarding single vs. multi-agent systems
- Custom autonomy taxonomy not mapped to international frameworks
- Underdeveloped guidance on the Directive on Automated Decision-Making application
- Missing guidance on memory/context persistence, model updates, and third-party procurement
- No mention of Official Languages Act or accessibility obligations

These gaps are noted as opportunities for improvement in future revisions, not as evidence that the guide's core claims are wrong.

## 10. Final Credibility Verdict

**Rating: HIGH**

This source is a highly credible official government publication. The Guide on the Use of Agentic Artificial Intelligence is authored by the Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat, a department with statutory authority over government digital policy and a demonstrated track record in AI governance since 2019. The guide is part of an established policy ecosystem that includes the Directive on Automated Decision-Making and the Guide on the Use of Generative AI, both of which corroborate its approach. The guide explicitly aligns with internationally recognized frameworks including the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, EU AI Act principles, and OWASP guidance, and an independent third-party review confirmed its conceptual accuracy and practical value. The tone is neutral and professional, funding is from public tax revenues with no commercial conflicts, and the source provides practical guidance, risk assessments, and case studies backed by cross-referenced policy instruments.

The report also notes some technical imprecisions identified by independent reviewers, notably regarding single- versus multi-agent definitions, the absence of guidance on memory persistence and model updates, and underdeveloped integration with the Directive on Automated Decision-Making. These gaps represent opportunities for improvement in future revisions, but they do not substantially undermine the credibility of an official government policy document that is corroborated by established frameworks and independent expert review.

## 11. Limitations and Unverified Information

The following limitations or unverified aspects were identified during the investigation:

1. **Individual author not named**: No individual author name is displayed on the page. This is standard for government guidance documents, but limits attribution transparency.

2. **Some technical gaps**: Independent reviewers identified areas where the guide could be more comprehensive — including guidance on memory/context persistence, model update versioning, third-party procurement, and multi-agent trust boundaries. These are noted as gaps, not evidence of inaccuracy.

3. **Preliminary guidance statement**: The guide states it "may be updated regularly" — meaning it represents the state of guidance at a point in time and may evolve.

4. **Full PDF version could not be fully read**: An archived PDF version of the guide was located but could not be fully processed. However, the content was accessible through the primary Canada.ca page and corroborated by independent sources.
